The brief, and the throughline

Everything below hangs off one sentence. If a beat doesn’t serve it, cut the beat.

Series throughline

A screw thread is a ratio, and a gearbox is just a very expensive, very inflexible way of holding one. Replace it with maths and the gears become optional — but only if the maths is right, and mine wasn’t, and neither was anybody else’s.

That second clause is what makes this a series rather than a build video. The honest story isn’t “I found a project and built it”. It’s “I found a project that worked on the author’s lathe with the author’s numbers, and every bug in it was hiding behind a default value.” That’s a story with a villain, and it earns the technical episodes.

Tone

  • Credit generously, early, and by name. Not an Engineer had the idea and shipped it. Jack From Scratch turned a sketch into software. You forked it and took it somewhere else. All three of those are true at once and the series is stronger for saying so plainly.
  • Show the failures at full size. The scrapped threads are better television than the good ones, and they are the reason to trust the good ones.
  • Never sell it as safe. An ELS removes a mechanical interlock and replaces it with your firmware. Say that out loud, more than once, without hedging.
  • No “and it just worked”. Nothing in this project just worked. Two of the bugs took an evening each and one of them made three confident, wrong diagnoses first.

The AI question, handled once and properly

You asked for honesty without letting it become the story, and there’s a factually clean way to do that because the timeline supports it. Say it in EP 2, in one 3–4 minute segment, and then don’t bring it up again except where it’s load-bearing.

The framing, and it is accurate to the repo

The motion engine you built and debugged yourself, on hardware, across 2025: the ESP32 port, the RMT pulse output, the dual-core work, the ratio and steps-per-mm fixes, and the sync-start optimisation — that last one is the original piece of engineering in this project, and it is dated May–June 2025, months before any AI is in the commit log.

From August 2026 the tooling changed: test-first specifications, a host-side screen renderer, characterisation suites, the UI rewrite. Growth from 54 host tests to 487, and from zero rendered screens to 41. That is what a power tool does to a workshop — it doesn’t design the piece, it removes the hours between deciding and knowing.

The line worth saying on camera: “It never wrote a line of the motion loop that I didn’t specify, test and read. What it did was make it cheap to write four hundred tests, and those tests found bugs that had been in this codebase since before I touched it.”

Why five parts, and in this order

You asked me to let the content decide. It decided on five — and the ordering matters more than the count.

Three would have worked, but the build content alone is two solid episodes: ordering and assembling a four-layer board with a soldered-down WROOM module is a different job from wiring a stepper to a lathe, and both are different again from flashing a device that has no USB socket and then telling it the truth about your leadscrew. Compressing those loses the people who actually want to build one, which is the whole conversion audience.

The order is chronological for the first two parts, then reference for the last three:

PartJob it doesEnds on
1 · OriginsExplain the problem, credit the lineage, make people want oneA thread being cut, no gears in the machine
2 · EngineeringThe bugs, the fork, the realtime work — earns your credibilityThe sync-start fix, set up as a cliffhanger for EP 3
3 · OperationThe masterclass. The one that gets rewatched and linkedThreading to a shoulder, stopping on the line
4 · HardwareConvert viewers into builders: board, motor, encoder, wiringA finished panel, unpowered
5 · CommissioningFlash it, configure it, prove it before you cutFirst correct thread on their lathe

The one non-obvious call: engineering before operation. It looks backwards — surely people want to see it work before they hear about heap corruption? But EP 1 already ends with it working, so the payoff is banked. And EP 2 ends on the acceleration problem, which is precisely the thing EP 3 opens by teaching you to machine around. That hand-off only works in this order.

Publishing

Weekly. Film EP 1 and EP 3 in the same session — both live at the lathe and share nearly every b-roll setup. Cut EP 1 first, publish, and let its comments tell you which EP 3 questions to answer. Nobody else will have built one of these while the series is in production, so EP 4 and EP 5 stand on your own build alone — which means being straight about the parts of it that are specific to your machine, rather than implying a tested-by-others kit.

What a leadscrew actually does

Hands on the machine: turn the chuck by hand, watch the carriage crawl. Then a clean animated diagram — spindle, gear train, leadscrew, half-nuts — with the ratio called out as a number.

A screw thread is a ratio. One turn of the spindle, and the tool has to move along by exactly one pitch. A millimetre and a half, or a twentieth of an inch, or whatever you’ve decided the thread is going to be.

And it has to be exactly that. Not nearly. Because you don’t cut a thread in one pass — you cut it in ten or fifteen, going a bit deeper each time. Get the ratio wrong by a fraction of a percent and you don’t get a slightly wrong thread. You get a groove that slowly wanders out of the one you cut on the last pass, and by the fourth pass you’ve got two threads on top of each other and a scrap part.

This is a change-gear train. Two gears out of a set in a tin, meshed between the spindle and the leadscrew — and the chart on the inside of the cover tells you which two, for which pitch.

That’s the catch, right there. You get the pitches on the chart. Not the pitch you want — the pitches somebody worked out for you, years ago, for that column of tooth counts. Want something that isn’t on the chart? You’re not changing gears any more. You’re doing the maths yourself.

And every change is the same fifteen minutes: cover off, gears out, the right pair in, mesh checked by feel, cover back on. Going from metric to imperial or back needs an extra gear just to make the conversion — and even then, that fifteen minutes doesn’t get you an exact pitch. Just a close one.

Half-nuts and the thread dial — the name drop

Close on the half-nut lever, engaging and disengaging. Then a thread dial with its numbered face — generic, filmed at the makerspace alongside B-02 (see below), because Martin’s own lathe doesn’t have one. Cut from that to Martin’s own half-nut lever, left engaged, and the spindle reversing.

Between the leadscrew and the carriage there’s a split nut — two halves that you clamp onto the screw to engage the feed. Half-nuts.

And the thread dial exists because of a genuinely awkward problem: once you disengage at the end of a pass and wind the carriage back, how do you know you’re going to drop back into the same groove? You watch the dial, and you engage on the same number every time.

But it gets worse. A thread dial is geared off the leadscrew, so it only reads correctly for one threading system — metric or imperial. Cut the other one on the same machine and the dial just lies to you.

And mine doesn’t have one at all. So the technique on this lathe was: never disengage. Leave the half-nuts clamped for the whole job, and wind the spindle backwards between passes instead — the carriage retraces the exact same helix going the other way, and you’re always back where you started. It works. It is also one more thing to get wrong under time pressure, on top of everything else this operation demands.

That’s the job this project deletes. The pitch stops being something you catch on a spinning dial — or reverse the spindle to avoid needing one — and becomes something held in software. Which is where the name comes from, incidentally — it’s called HalfNut, because the half-nuts are the part it makes redundant.

The ELS idea, in one sentence

The key animation of the whole series — build it once, reuse it in EP 2 and EP 3. Gear train dissolves. Encoder appears on the spindle, stepper on the leadscrew, controller between them. Then show the loop running: spindle angle in, leadscrew position out.

Take the gear train out entirely. Put an encoder on the spindle, so the controller knows exactly where the spindle is — not how fast it’s going, where it is, to about a third of a degree. Put a stepper motor on the leadscrew.

And then, tens of thousands of times a second, ask one question: given where the spindle is right now, where should the leadscrew be? If that’s not where it currently is, send it a pulse.

That’s it. That is the entire idea. Every thread pitch on the chart, and every one that isn’t, becomes a number in a table.

Accuracy note: this is a genuine description of the firmware. The leadscrew is driven by position error, not by speed matching. Worth being precise about, because it’s the thing that makes EP 2 make sense.

Bug 2 — steps per millimetre forgot the gearbox

Your actual 2:1 reduction, filmed close. It is a gear reduction and the shot must make that obvious — a machined adapter carrying one of the lathe’s own change gears on the stepper shaft, meshed with another change gear on the leadscrew. Then the arithmetic on screen, worked slowly: motor steps → motor turns → reduction → leadscrew turns → millimetres.

The steps-per-millimetre calculation didn’t include the gearbox ratio at all. Which is completely invisible if you’ve coupled your stepper straight onto the leadscrew, because then the ratio is one, and multiplying by one is famously hard to spot.

I hadn’t. I’ve got a two-to-one gear reduction. I machined an adapter so I could put one of the lathe’s own change gears on the stepper shaft and mesh it with another on the leadscrew — two turns of the motor for one turn of the screw. That buys the motor a mechanical advantage, which is what lets it push a real depth of cut without complaining.

And it has to be gears, or a toothed belt. Not a vee belt, not a friction drive. Anything that can slip even slightly has no business between a stepper and a leadscrew, because the whole premise is that the software knows where the carriage is.

So my machine moved exactly half as far as it thought it did — and everything downstream of that number was wrong too. Feed rates. Acceleration limits. The stopping distance it used to decide when to start slowing down for the end stop.

The evening it dropped to 58 Hz

The best story in the series. Tell it as a detective story and do not give the answer early.

The motion loop runs at seventy-eight thousand iterations a second. One evening, while jogging, it was running at fifty-eight. Not fifty-eight thousand. Fifty-eight.

The step pulses go out through a peripheral called the RMT, which takes an array of pulse definitions. And the call that hands it that array takes a count. I’d passed it sizeof — the size of the array in bytes. Which made it ninety-six, for an array of twenty-four. So it read seventy-two entries past the end of the array, and it also blew past the hardware buffer size, so the write blocked while the peripheral clocked out uninitialised heap.

And here’s the part that nearly broke me: only the first element of that array had ever been initialised. So what it was actually transmitting was whatever happened to be sitting in memory after it — which rearranges itself every time you rebuild. The same commit measured fast on one build and catastrophically slow on the next.

Land it hard: you bisected across four commits and produced three confident, wrong conclusions. What actually solved it was putting a counter on the loop and reading the number.

Bug 4, paid off — the sync start

That is the problem named, the mechanism named — “closes the position error in the fastest possible time allowable with the max speed” is the velocity overshoot — and the fix promised, all before it existed. Put it on screen, then cut to the animation. It turns the beat from an explanation into a payoff.

The centrepiece — already built. Cut g2a_problem (19 s), then g2b_fix (15 s) on identical axes, so the fix visibly fills the empty half of the frame that the problem leaves behind. Both carry a velocity panel underneath the position panel — that lower graph is where the argument actually lives, so hold on it. In g2a the speed climbs past the thread speed and pins to the ceiling; in g2b it walks up to thread speed and stays flat. See §04.

Here’s the problem. When you press ENABLE, the spindle is already turning and the leadscrew is stopped. You can’t just start moving — a motor with a lathe carriage on the end of it has to accelerate.

So the original code did the obvious thing: wait until the spindle comes round to the sync angle, and start there. Which means at the instant it starts, it is perfectly correct — and a fraction of a second later it’s behind, because it spent that fraction of a second getting up to speed. Then it notices it’s behind, so it runs faster than the thread to catch up. Then it overshoots.

Let me put real numbers on it. Two-and-a-half millimetre pitch, eight hundred RPM. By the time it is up to speed it has fallen 3.7 millimetres behind — and that is exactly the distance it takes to accelerate, which is not a coincidence, it is the same sum.

Now. How do you close a gap like that? There is only one way. You run faster than the thread. There is no other mechanism available — the carriage has to overtake the helix to get back onto it.

And on this machine it runs straight into the speed ceiling and sits there: forty millimetres a second against a thread speed of thirty-three. Twenty per cent over. Which sounds abstract until you convert it, because carriage speed divided by spindle speed is pitch — and forty millimetres a second at eight hundred RPM is a three millimetre pitch. On a two-and-a-half millimetre thread.

It holds that for eight tenths of a second before it is back on the helix. Twenty-seven millimetres of travel. So every pass starts too fine, while it is accelerating, and then goes too coarse, while it catches up — and both of those are cut into the work.

Beat. Then the fix, on the same animation, replayed.

The fix isn’t to accelerate harder. It’s to start earlier.

I know the acceleration rate, because it’s a setting. I know the target speed, because I’m measuring the spindle continuously. So I can work out exactly how far the carriage travels while it’s getting up to speed — it’s just the area under that ramp. Half the speed difference, times the time it takes.

So instead of asking “is the spindle at the sync angle now”, it asks: “will the spindle be at the sync angle by the time I’ve finished accelerating?” Start the ramp there, and the carriage arrives at full speed exactly on the helix. No catch-up. No overshoot. It is in sync from the first correct millimetre.

That, for my money, is the single best thing in this firmware, and it’s the one bit that’s properly mine. May 2025.

The bit nobody tells you — run-in and run-out

The episode’s centrepiece — already built. g2c_workpiece (12 s) puts the same distance on the part: a bar in side elevation, chuck at the left, three zones shaded as the tool passes — accelerating, in sync, decelerating — with both dimensions called out as the same number. See §04.

Last episode I explained that the controller starts its acceleration ramp early, so that it arrives at full speed exactly on the helix. That’s the good news. Here’s the bill.

It can only start early if there is somewhere to start. The carriage physically has to travel that run-up distance before the thread begins — so you have to start the pass beyond the end of the thread. Not level with it. Beyond it.

And it’s the same at the other end, for exactly the same reason in reverse. When it approaches the stop it has to decelerate, and during that deceleration it is no longer following the helix. So the last few millimetres before the stop are not a good thread either.

Run-in and run-out are the same distance, and it’s the same sum: speed squared, over twice the acceleration.

The bits that stop you hurting yourself

Quick tour, 90 seconds: while the carriage is moving the panel answers only HALT and ENABLE, and any open panel closes itself — your attention belongs on the tool · the DRO is always referenced to an end stop, never to “wherever it booted”, and it flashes when the datum moves · and if the stepper driver raises an alarm, the machine halts, latches, and stays latched until you acknowledge it — because a fault that trips and clears still moved the carriage, and the sync is now worthless.

Three things, ninety seconds, and then we’re done with safety mechanics.

While the carriage is moving, the panel answers exactly two keys: halt, and enable. Nothing else. Any menu that happens to be open closes itself. Your attention belongs on the tool, not on a settings screen, and the panel enforces that whether you meant to leave it open or not.

The DRO is always referenced to an end stop — never to wherever the controller happened to boot up. And it flashes whenever that datum changes, so you can’t miss it moving under you.

And if the stepper driver raises an alarm, the machine halts, latches, and stays latched until you acknowledge it. Not because a fault that trips and clears is dangerous by itself — it’s that the carriage moved while it was tripped, and the moment it moved, your sync stopped being trustworthy. Acknowledging isn’t clearing the fault. It’s you confirming you know the sync is gone.

On a lathe it means the controller’s idea of where the carriage is — which is the only thing keeping the tool out of the chuck — is now quietly wrong, and it will stay wrong.

A closed-loop drive has an encoder on the motor. It knows when it has fallen behind, it corrects it, and when it can’t, it raises an alarm on a wire. Correct the obvious assumption before someone comments

The drive between spindle and encoder does not have to be 1:1. Mine is not. I mounted the encoder on an existing take-off point, and there is an internal gear train giving four spindle turns per encoder turn.

What it must be is positive and non-slipping — gears or a toothed belt, never friction. A fixed reduction is simply compensated in the spindle-PPR setting: mine is set to a quarter of the encoder’s own count, so the number the firmware holds is counts per spindle revolution, which is what it actually wants.

One caveat worth stating, because it bites silently: counts per spindle revolution has to come out a whole number. Four into a quadrature count divides cleanly. A three-to-one reduction on a 1000-line encoder does not — and the leftover fraction accumulates as thread phase error over a long pass. Backlash in that train only matters across a spindle reversal, which is not something you do mid-thread.

The arithmetic itself is EP 5’s job. Here, just plant it: “the number the controller wants is counts per turn of the spindle, not per turn of the encoder — and if there is anything geared in between, those are different numbers.”

Push it to 1,600 and you are spending two-thirds of that budget on the fastest jog you will ever ask for. Past roughly 2,500 on this machine’s own numbers, the configured max speed simply cannot be reached — the loop is not stalling, it is out of pulses to send. Work the same arithmetic on your own pitch, gearbox ratio and max speed before chasing smoothness: (max speed ÷ leadscrew pitch) × gearbox ratio × stepper PPR, and it either fits under your loop’s ceiling or it doesn’t.

The 78 kHz figure and the one-pulse-per-loop-iteration behaviour are both sourced from lib/leadscrew/leadscrew.cpp (Leadscrew::sendPulse() and update()) — re-verify against the firmware running at recording time, the same as every other number in this reference segment.

Also explain the split, because it’s a design decision worth defending: geometry is web-only and the device never writes it — it’s commissioning data, set once with the lathe switched off. Preferences that are worth changing mid-job — theme, DRO datum, units, jog speed — live on the menu, and even those are refused while the carriage is under power.

Proving it — before any tool touches anything

Do this on camera, in order, as a checklist people can follow. Leadscrew disconnected for the first two. Put the list on screen as you go.

Five checks, in order, before a tool goes anywhere near the work. First two with the leadscrew physically disconnected — I mean that, not just powered down.

I’ll read each one out as I do it, so you can follow along and run this exact sequence on your own machine the first time you power yours up.

  1. Direction. Jog left. Does it go left? If not, invert direction — don’t start rewiring.
  2. Distance. Dial indicator on the carriage. Jog a known distance. Does the DRO agree with the indicator? If it’s out by a clean factor, you have a steps-per-mm or microstepping problem, not a mechanical one.
  3. Encoder. One full spindle turn by hand, watching Diagnostics. This is not a check, it is the measurement — whatever it reads is your spindle encoder PPR, gearing and quadrature included.
  4. Pitch, without cutting. Reconnect. Set a coarse pitch. Turn the spindle by hand for ten full turns and measure how far the carriage moved. Ten turns × pitch. It either matches or it doesn’t, and this costs you nothing but a minute.
  5. Then cut a test thread in something soft and put a nut on it.

This checklist is the single most useful two minutes in the series for someone who has just built one. Make it screenshot-friendly and put it in the description as text.

The camera, and what it decides

Google Pixel 9, shooting 4K at 30 fps. Cut 1920×1080 at 30. The Panasonic TZ7 (720p, 25 fps, 15-minute clips, no mic input) drops back to an occasional second angle.

30, not 25. The Pixel offers 24/30/60 and does not expose 25, and it handles mains-flicker anti-banding itself — so the 50 Hz argument for 25 does not apply to this camera. Forcing 25 would mean a third-party app and losing Pixel’s computational video, which is a bad trade. Everything downstream is already at 30: the thirteen animations need no re-render, and resolve/out/30fps/ is the marker set to import.

Shoot 4K into a 1080 timeline — the most useful single decision here. It downscales very sharp, and a punch-in can be cut as a second angle from one take, which substantially solves the one-camera problem below. Frame every wide with room to crop. Shoot SDR, not HDR, and lock focus and exposure before each take: an autofocus rack mid-pass ruins it.

Standing conditions for every lathe setup

  • Audio — one transmitter, so it follows the sound. The DJI Mic Mini 2S TX records internally in 32-bit float, which is what a lathe needs: the dynamic range is unrehearsable and float cannot clip recoverably. Put it at the machine for cold opens and clean cutting passes; on you for pieces to camera and for live demos while it runs, where the lav is a metre from the cut and the machine bleed reads as authentic. Sync by waveform in Resolve; clap as belt-and-braces.
  • One pass becomes the audio pass. Coverage already needs the shot more than once, so shoot one repeat with the TX at the machine and nobody speaking — that is the cold-open and b-roll sound. It is a role given to a pass you are shooting anyway, and it works because the machine repeats a threading pass identically. Worth one line on camera.
  • Guard the TX at the machine. A 12 g clip-on beside something throwing hot swarf and slinging oil. Headstock or a stand, off-axis from the chip stream, never where it could be drawn toward the chuck — and check the capsule between setups.
  • Guards. Several shots want the guard open so the carriage and leadscrew are visible. Do those with the spindle stopped wherever the shot allows, and say on camera that the guard is open for the camera — otherwise the comments will do it for you.
  • Continuity. Same workpiece stock, same tool, same chuck jaws across a setup. Cutting oil looks different wet and dry; re-wet between takes.
  • Coverage — one camera, plus the crop. Every cutting shot wants a wide, an over-shoulder on the panel, and a macro on the tool. A 4K master supplies the wide and a punch-in; the macro still needs its own pass. The ELS makes a threading pass genuinely repeatable, so this costs takes rather than quality — and it is worth one line on camera that the repeatability is what made the filming possible.
  • Storage. 4K30 runs about 350 MB per minute. A full Setup A or Setup C day is comfortably 100 GB — check free space before you start, not after.
  • The camera cannot carry the screen. An over-shoulder shot of a 320×240 panel at 720p will be marginal. Anything the viewer must read comes from render.sh; the camera supplies context only. This was already the plan — now it is non-negotiable.

Re-rendering, and changing the numbers

Everything derives from four constants at the top of anim/scenes.py: PITCH_MM, RPM, ACCEL and VMAX. Change them and every trace, dimension and caption recomputes — nothing is hand-drawn or hard-coded.

These are now confirmed as your machine, not “the defaults”. Your configuration is the shipped default throughout — 150 mm/s² acceleration, 40 mm/s max, 2.54 mm leadscrew, 2:1 gears, 400 step motor, 1200 counts per spindle revolution — so every number spoken in EP 2 and EP 3, and every dimension drawn in these clips, is a measurement of the lathe in the shots. Nothing needs re-rendering, and you can say “this is my machine” rather than “at typical settings”, which is a stronger line anyway.

One modelling note, in case it is ever questioned: the naive trace comes from a bang-bang position controller, and a pure one limit-cycles forever after it catches up. The firmware does not, because it compares integer pulse positions and therefore has a natural deadband — so the simulation has one too (DEADBAND, 0.05 mm). Without it the velocity panel ends in a sawtooth that is an artefact of the model, not a behaviour of your lathe.

Building these changed the script — check this before you record EP 2

The first version of these graphs only plotted position, and on that evidence I told you the overshoot was negligible. That was wrong, and it was wrong because I was measuring the wrong quantity — the displacement overshoot really is tiny (0.155 mm), but the overshoot that matters is in speed, and it is not tiny at all. Both scenes now carry a velocity panel, and it is the better graph of the two.

What it shows: the carriage falls 3.70 mm behind — exactly v²/2a, a genuinely satisfying identity — and the only way to close that is to overtake the helix. It pins to the firmware’s 40 mm/s ceiling against a 33.3 mm/s thread speed. That is 20% over, held for 808 ms and 26.9 mm.

And because carriage speed divided by spindle speed is pitch, that converts to a number a machinist can act on: it is cutting a 3.0 mm pitch on a 2.5 mm thread. Which restores the original framing exactly — too fine during the ramp, too coarse during the catch-up.

Both numbers move with your settings. The 20% is set by the gap between your max-speed setting and the thread speed — raise the ceiling and the overshoot gets worse in magnitude but shorter in duration; lower it and the recovery drags on. Worth saying on camera, because it means there is no setting that fixes it. Only starting early does.

Still worth building, and not yet built: the full wiring diagram assembled one connection at a time (EP 4, G4). g4b_encoder covers the encoder half of it and was traced from the PCB netlist rather than written from memory; the power and RJ45 half still wants the same treatment. The Resolve pipeline

Resolve Free exposes no scripting API, so nothing can be driven from outside the application. What it does import is markers — and that turns out to be the part worth automating anyway.

Built and waiting in docs/video-series/resolve/out/

Ten EDLs — one per episode at 30 fps (the set to use) and again at 25 fps — plus beats.csv for anything that is not Resolve. Every beat in all five scripts becomes a marker, coloured by shot type and given a duration that runs to the next beat, so the timeline shows a colour-coded structural spine rather than a row of dots. 67 markers in total.

They are generated from storyboard.html itself, so they cannot drift from the script. Re-run python make_edl.py after any beat change.

Importing them

  1. Create the timeline for the episode first, at your project frame rate.
  2. In the Media Pool, right-click that timeline.
  3. Timelines → Import → Timeline Markers from EDL.
  4. Pick the matching file — out/30fps/ep3_markers_30fps.edl for this production. The 25 fps set is kept in case the plan changes.

If nothing appears, the timeline’s start timecode does not match the EDL’s. The files are written for Resolve’s default 01:00:00:00 start; if your timelines start at zero, regenerate with python make_edl.py --start 00:00:00:00. Zoom out before concluding they failed.

Audio sync, and one thing to test before a shoot day

The DJI transmitter records internally, so the good audio never depends on the wireless link or on the phone. Bring the WAVs in alongside the video, auto-sync by waveform (in Resolve Free), then mute the camera track.

Test once, in advance: does the Pixel’s stock camera app actually accept audio from the USB-C receiver? Android USB-audio routing into camera apps is inconsistent. If it does not, let the phone record its own mic purely as a sync guide — that path is more robust anyway, so treat it as the default rather than the fallback.
